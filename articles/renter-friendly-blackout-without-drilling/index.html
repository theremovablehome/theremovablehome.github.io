Renter-friendly blackout without drilling a single hole

How to block streetlights and early sun in a rental using layered curtains, portable shades, and no-drill rods — including what actually works for shift sleepers.

A rental bedroom can be bright for reasons that have nothing to do with your taste in lamps: a streetlight aimed at the pillow, a neighbor’s security flood, thin builder blinds, or a sunrise that hits the bed at 5:20 a.m. If you also sleep during the day — night shifts, split shifts, a new baby, a migraine — “room-darkening” is not a style preference. It is how you get rest.

Drilling a professional blackout shade into the casing is the cleanest permanent solution. It is also the solution most leases are written to discourage. This guide covers the no-drill stack that actually gets a room dark enough to sleep in, and it is honest about the leftover glow you may still see at the edges.

If you have not chosen hardware yet, start with how to hang curtains without drilling. This page assumes you already understand tension rods, twist-and-fit rods, and adhesive hooks, and need those tools to do a harder job.

Why most blackout curtains disappoint

True blackout fabric does not glow when you hold it in front of a lamp. Plenty of panels labeled “blackout” are really room-darkening: they take the edge off, then leak around every side. In a rental the leaks are worse because no-drill hardware often sits inside the frame, which leaves the wall around the window uncovered.

Light enters a bedroom through five paths:

  1. Through the fabric
  2. Through the gap between two panels
  3. Around the left and right edges
  4. Over the top of the rod
  5. Under the hem, bouncing off a pale floor or up from blinds

Fixing only path 1 — buying thicker fabric — and ignoring the other four is how people end up writing reviews that say blackout curtains “don’t work.” The curtains worked. The installation left the room outlined in daylight.

Step 1: Make the existing blinds useful

Many rentals require you to leave the horizontal blinds in place. Do not fight them on day one. Close them fully, tilt the slats upward (which often blocks more light than tilting down), and see what remains. If slats are bent or missing, photograph them for your move-in log before you cover anything; you do not want to inherit someone else’s broken slat as a deposit argument.

Blinds alone almost never produce shift-worker darkness. They do cut glare and give you a fallback if you take the curtains down to open the window wide.

Step 2: Choose fabric that is actually opaque

Hold a phone flashlight behind the fabric in the store, or do the same test as soon as a package arrives. If you can read the screen through the panel, it is not blackout.

A pair of true blackout curtain panels in a dark or mid tone will hide light better than a pale linen-look blackout that still seems to glow at the edges. Color is not the whole story — a well-made pale blackout layer can work — but dark fabrics forgive small leaks.

Weight matters because your hardware is doing extra work. If a tension rod is your only option, you may need to split the difference: a slightly lighter room-darkening panel plus a second layer (see below) rather than one quilted drape that walks the rod out of the frame.

Step 3: Cover more than the glass

This is the step that changes results.

For an outside mount (adhesive hooks or wrap-around hardware), extend the curtains several inches past each side of the window and a few inches above the frame if the wall allows it. The extra width lets you stack fabric in front of the wall, not just in front of the glass.

For an inside mount (tension rod), you cannot cover the wall. You can still:

  • Use a wrap-style or twist-and-fit rod that lets fabric turn the corner
  • Add a second, shorter tension rod on each side if the frame is deep enough for the panels to return toward the wall
  • Choose a rod that sits as close to the glass as the blinds allow, so there is less “light box” behind the fabric

Floor-length panels help with bounce from below. If a radiator or a vent forbids that, a draft stopper or fabric door snake along the sill or the floor line will cut a surprising amount of glow.

Step 4: Add a second layer where one layer fails

Serious darkness is usually a stack, not a single purchase.

Useful combinations in a rental:

  • Builder blinds + blackout curtains
  • Blackout curtains + a portable blackout shade used only during sleep hours
  • Curtains + static-cling darkness or glare film on the lower sash (film alone will not black out a room; see the privacy film guide)
  • A temporary shade on the bedroom window and lighter curtains in the rest of the apartment, so you are not asking a living-room tension rod to do night-shift work

Portable shades — the kind that mount with suction, hook-and-loop strips sold as removable, or a spring tension system sized to the glass — can seal better than drapes because they treat the window as a rectangle to be covered, not as a decoration. The tradeoffs are real: they need an accurate measurement, cheap suction fails on dirty or cold glass, and you must confirm the adhesive is one you can reverse. Read the removal instructions the way you would for wallpaper.

If you share a bedroom or you want the room to look normal during waking hours, a portable shade you put up at bedtime is often less visually heavy than living full-time behind bunker drapes.

Shift sleepers and anyone asleep during the day

Daytime sleep is harder than nighttime sleep because the light source is the sun, not a streetlamp, and it moves. A system that looked perfect at 10 p.m. can glow at noon around a rod that seemed tight enough.

Extra steps that help:

  • Test the room at the actual time you will sleep, not only after sunset.
  • Cover the edges, then stand where your pillow is. The pillow view is what matters.
  • Darken the gap under the door with a draft stopper if hallway light is part of the problem.
  • Use an eye mask as a backup, not as the entire plan; jaw tension and hot apartments make masks a poor only tool.
  • Keep the window operable. You still need air and, in many bedrooms, a legal emergency exit. A portable shade should come down quickly. Never screw a window shut or block it with furniture you cannot move while half asleep.

A small white-noise machine will not darken a room, but for day sleepers the same street that sends in light also sends in traffic. Darkness and sound are separate problems; solving only one of them still feels like a failure.

What not to do

Permanent foil or contractor paper taped to the glass with packing tape. It works in a pinch and can leave adhesive on the glass, peel window film the landlord installed, or look alarming from the sidewalk. If you need a temporary cover for one week, use painter’s tape on the frame — not the glass — and a piece of foam board cut to the opening, then replace it with a real system.

Adhesive hooks on hot window casing in afternoon sun. Heat weakens a lot of foam adhesives. If the only wall available bakes at 3 p.m., pick a tension method that does not rely on glue.

Warning the paint off with extra-strength hooks so you can hang two layers of hotel-weight drapes. If the fabric is that heavy, you have outgrown no-drill hooks. Ask about proper brackets or switch to a portable shade.

Black trash bags. They are a moving-week tactic, not a bedroom.

A realistic shopping list

For one ordinary bedroom window:

  1. Measure the glass and the inside frame.
  2. Buy true blackout curtain panels wide enough for your mount style.
  3. Buy the sturdiest no-drill rod that fits, usually a heavy-duty tension rod or a twist-and-fit rod.
  4. If a test night still shows a halo around the frame, add a portable blackout shade or side returns.
  5. Optional: a draft stopper for the door or sill.

Do the flashlight test and a real sleep test before you buy a second set of hardware. Most rooms need better coverage, not a more expensive fabric with the same gaps.

When the room still will not go dark

Some apartments have a glass door, a transom, a west-facing wall of windows, or a skylight you are not allowed to cover. In those cases, aim your budget at the sleep zone: a portable shade on the window closest to the bed, an eye mask as backup, and a conversation with the landlord about one reversible shade in the bedroom. Document the request and any approval in writing.

Blackout is one of the few rental upgrades that pays you back every day you live there. It is also one of the easiest to overbuy. Cover the glass, then the edges, then stop.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get true blackout in a rental without drilling?

Yes, close enough for sleep, if you treat the edges as part of the project. Most “blackout curtains” fail because of side and top light leaks, not because the fabric itself glows.

Are portable blackout shades better than curtains?

They can be, especially on a single bedroom window, because they seal to the frame. They are more fiddly to take up and down every day and they have to be sized carefully.

Will window film black out a room?

Decorative privacy film will not. Even dark film rarely produces a sleep-dark room by itself and can change how the glass looks from outside. Use film for glare or privacy, then add a fabric layer for night.
