First-week apartment setup checklist (before you hang anything)

A renter’s first-week checklist: deposit photos, safety checks, a small sleep-and-kitchen kit, and the no-drill upgrades you should wait a week to buy.

How to read this guide. Recommendations are based on product specs, manufacturer removal instructions, and common failure modes — not a claim that we personally lab-tested every SKU mentioned. The first week in a rental is when people either protect their deposit or accidentally decorate themselves into a project. The useful sequence is almost the opposite of what social media suggests. You do not start with an accent wall. You start with proof of how the place looked when you arrived, then with sleep, then with the smallest set of objects that make the kitchen and bathroom function.

This checklist is the narrative version of The Removable Home Starter Kit, a printable PDF with the same photo log, wall audit, and budget lists. Use either; use both if you like checking boxes on paper.

Day one: document, then unpack

Before the boxes climb the stairs, walk the unit with your phone as if you were the person who will later argue about a scuffed fridge handle.

Photograph:

  • Every wall in every room, including closets
  • Ceilings, especially corners (old leaks show up as stains)
  • Baseboards, vents, and window sills
  • Blinds, screens, and the way each window locks
  • Floors in raking light, where scratches appear
  • Inside the oven, fridge, drawers, and dishwasher if there is one
  • Under every sink, including the cabinet floor
  • The bathroom ceiling, caulk lines, fan, and around the toilet base
  • Any existing holes, command-strip shadows, or unmatched paint
  • Meter readings or thermostat settings if they matter in your building
  • The keys, fobs, and parking passes you were handed

Do this in daylight as well as with the overhead lights on. Timestamped photos in a single album named with the address-free label “move-in” are enough; you do not need to publish them. If something is broken — a window that does not lock, a missing smoke alarm, a dishwasher full of someone else’s food — send that to the landlord or manager before you settle in, using the photos.

Only after the album exists should you lean the mattress against a wall and start opening boxes. If you cover a mark with a tapestry on night one, you may never remember whether it was yours.

Day one: a safety and function pass

Walk the unit again with a narrower question: can I sleep here tonight without inventing a hazard?

  • Confirm the door latches and deadbolts work. If a lock is flaky, that is a same-day message, not a decorative problem.
  • Check that smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are present where they should be. Do not remove them. If they are missing or chirping on a dying battery, report it.
  • Locate the breaker panel and the water shutoff so you are not learning during a problem.
  • Test the windows you would use as an emergency exit in a bedroom. They should open. Your future blackout plan cannot require nailing a sash shut.
  • Replace a missing shower curtain immediately with a liner on a spring tension rod so water stays in the tub. A flooded floor on night one is an expensive way to meet the downstairs neighbor.

A cheap plug-in night light in an unfamiliar hallway prevents the other classic first-night injury: a meeting between a toe and a box.

The 48-hour buying list (and nothing else)

If the apartment is empty, you need a very short list. If you already own dishes and bedding, you need even less.

Sleep

  • Sheets, a pillow, and a way to block the worst of the streetlight — even a temporary layer — so you can think the next day
  • Earplugs if the building is louder than the listing implied

Wash

  • Toilet paper, soap, a towel, a shower liner, a tension rod if the stall has no working rod
  • A basic plunger. Every apartment needs one; not every apartment admits it.

Eat

  • One pot or pan, one knife, one cutting board, a spoon, a mug, dish soap, a sponge
  • A refillable water bottle if you do not trust the first night of unpacking to produce a glass

Clean

  • Trash bags, an all-purpose cleaner that you wipe off (so it does not become its own residue problem), paper towels or washable cloths
  • A broom or vacuum depending on the floors

Hang, maybe

  • Two standard adhesive hooks for a towel and a robe if the paint looks cured and smooth, installed according to the hook guide
  • Otherwise a freestanding hook or rack

That is enough to be a person in the space. It is not enough to finish the space, and it is not supposed to be.

Days two to four: learn the light and the walls

Live in the apartment before you improve it. Notice where the afternoon sun hits, where the entry piles up, whether you can see in from the sidewalk at night, and whether the bedroom gets a streetlight across the pillow.

Do a wall and surface audit with your hand as well as your eyes:

  • Smooth painted drywall, or texture you can feel?
  • Paint that rubs off on a finger (chalky) or feels sound?
  • Fresh-paint smell?
  • Tile that is glossy and flat, or textured and handmade-looking?
  • Window frames of wood, vinyl, or metal?
  • Any existing wallpaper?

Write this down. It determines whether peel-and-stick backsplash, wallpaper, hooks, and privacy film are even on the table.

This is also the moment to measure windows — inside width at three points, inside height, frame depth — so you do not order a tension curtain rod that is theoretically adjustable and practically too short.

Days five to seven: one comfort upgrade at a time

Pick the problem that affected your sleep or your dignity the most, not the problem that would photograph best.

Typical first upgrades that stay lease-safe:

  1. Privacy or blackout on the window that bothers you, using no-drill curtain methods or privacy film
  2. Bathroom storage that matches the actual tile, from the bathroom guide
  3. Closet capacity via a second closet tension rod rather than a new wardrobe that fills the studio
  4. A rolling cart if the kitchen has no drawers, as in the studio storage plan

Defer:

  • Whole-wall wallpaper
  • A full kitchen backsplash
  • A portable AC if you moved in during mild weather and have not felt a true heat week yet (when heat does arrive, use the portable AC guide instead of panic-buying the first machine on a same-day list)
  • Anything that requires you to invent a new hole and explain it later

Three budget levels

About $50: function. Tension shower rod and liner, two hooks or a small rack, trash bags and cleaner, night light, a cafe curtain if a sidewalk window is the emergency.

About $150: comfort. Add a proper pair of curtains and a rod that fits, under-bed bins or a slim cart, and a better sleep setup if the first night proved the blinds useless.

About $400: it starts to feel like yours. Add the larger no-drill project — a carefully chosen backsplash or an accent wall, not both in the same week — plus bathroom storage that is freestanding or well matched to the tile, and the blackout stack if you need darkness to work or heal.

A note you can send if something is wrong

Keep landlord messages short, dated, and attached to photos. You do not need a speech. “The bedroom window lock does not catch, photo attached, please advise,” is enough. Charm upgrades can wait until the lock is a known issue, not your secret.

You are not behind if, at the end of week one, the walls are still beige and the only new hardware is a tension rod. You are ahead if you have a photo album, a working shower curtain, a place to sleep, and a written sense of what the walls will tolerate. The rest of this site is there when you are ready to make the place feel like yours without forgetting that you will someday leave it.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first on move-in day?

Photograph every room, closet, appliance, window treatment, and under-sink area before you unpack. That album is how you separate old damage from anything that happens later.

What are the only things I need to buy in the first 48 hours?

A way to sleep, a way to wash, a way to eat one simple meal, a basic cleaning kit, and anything that makes the place immediately safer — lighting in a dark entry, a working shower curtain on a tension rod, a door that actually latches.

When should I buy peel-and-stick tile or wallpaper?

After you have lived in the light of the apartment for several days and photographed the original walls. First-week energy is how people cover a wall they later realize they did not need to touch.
