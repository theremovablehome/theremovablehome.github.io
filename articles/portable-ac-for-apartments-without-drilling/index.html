Portable and window air conditioners that will not violate a careful lease

How to cool a rental with a portable or window air conditioner without drilling the frame, plus sizing, window kits, slider windows, noise, and security.

Upper-floor rentals in old buildings can feel like a rehearsal for a different climate. Ceiling fans help. Closed blinds help. At some point you still need cold air, and you need it without hanging a metal box from a sash the lease says you cannot alter.

This guide compares portable air conditioners and window units from a renter’s point of view: what the lease might care about, how to vent without screws, how to size the machine, and how not to turn the window into an unsecured opening. It is not a laboratory shootout of every BTU rating on the market. It is the decision framework that keeps you from buying a loud machine that fights itself.

Always read your lease and any building rules before you buy. Some properties ban window units, limit amperage, or require that a unit be installed by their staff. A written yes is worth more than a forum comment.

Portable AC versus window AC in a rental

A portable air conditioner sits on the floor and vents hot air through a hose into a window kit. A mid-size portable air conditioner is usually the path of least resistance for renters because the machine is inside the room and the window kit is designed to be temporary.

A window air conditioner sits in the opening, with most of its weight on the sill and its heat dumped directly outside. Window units are often more efficient for the same cooling power and quieter inside the room, because the noisy half lives outside. They also change the profile of the window, may require brackets or screws, and are the type many leases mention by name.

A third option, a saddle or U-shaped window unit, hugs the sash so the window can close through a gap in the chassis. Some renters like them because more of the noise sits outside and the sash still locks down. They still occupy the window and may be treated as a window unit in a strict lease.

If the lease is silent and the windows are ordinary vertical hung sashes, a portable unit with a no-screw kit is the conservative starting point. If the lease clearly allows a window unit and you want efficiency, a window unit with a proper support bracket — which may itself need approval — can be the better machine.

Sizing without superstition

BTU charts are blunt, but they keep people from cooling a studio with a machine meant for a closet, or from parking an enormous unit in a room where it short-cycles.

As a starting point for a typical insulated room with an eight-foot ceiling:

  • About 150–250 square feet: around 8,000 BTU
  • About 250–400 square feet: around 10,000 BTU
  • About 400–550 square feet: around 12,000 BTU

Then adjust with judgment. A top-floor west-facing studio with big glass and a kitchen that shares the air will want more capacity than a shaded interior bedroom. A machine that is wildly oversized may cool the thermostat quickly and leave humidity behind. A machine that is too small will run forever and still lose at 4 p.m.

Measure the room, not the whole apartment, if you only need to make the bedroom survivable. Cooling one room well is more realistic than cooling a railroad flat with one hose.

Also look at the plug. Many portables want a dedicated wall outlet, not a power strip, and some draw enough current to trip an older apartment circuit if a microwave and an iron are on the same line. If the listing says “220V,” it is not for a standard US bedroom receptacle.

The window kit, without screws

Almost every portable unit ships with accordion panels you cut to the width of the sash opening. The hose docks to a hole in that panel. This can be a no-drill install:

  1. Open the window, set the panel in the gap, and close the sash down onto it (or slide a horizontal slider against it).
  2. Fill leftover cracks with the included foam or extra window weatherstripping foam.
  3. Use the window’s existing lock if it still meets, or add an adjustable window security bar that tensions inside the frame without screws.
  4. Keep the hose as short and as straight as the room allows. Extra length is extra hot air sitting in a tube that radiates back into the room.

If the printed instructions mention screwing the panel into the frame, treat that as optional from a renter’s perspective. Friction, foam, and a sash closed firmly onto the kit are the usual alternative. Do not screw into aluminum or vinyl frames to save five minutes.

Horizontal slider and casement windows need a vertical kit. A vertical sliding window kit for portable ACs exists because a kit designed for an up-and-down sash will not honestly fill a side-sliding opening. Casements that crank outward are the hardest case; some people vent through a panel in a single open pane, which is a custom, drafty, and security-sensitive project. If your only windows are casements, confirm a plan before the heat wave, not during it.

Security. A window opened for a hose is a window that is no longer in its original locked position. Foam does not stop a determined person. A tensioned security bar, secondary sash lock that does not require drilling, and not venting through a ground-floor window hidden by bushes are all part of the install. This is especially true on first floors and fire escapes.

Single-hose versus dual-hose

A single-hose portable AC sucks air from the room, uses it to cool the machine, and dumps that air outside. The room then pulls replacement air from under the door and from any leak — often warm hallway air. That is why some units run constantly and the room still feels muggy.

A dual-hose unit pulls outside air through one hose to cool the machine and exhausts through the other, which is less at war with the room. Dual-hose machines are bulkier and often cost more. If you are cooling a studio where the only window is also your source of fresh air, the extra hose can be worth it.

In either design, empty the water the way the manual describes. Many modern units evaporate most condensate and only need a drain in very humid weather. “Self-evaporating” is not the same as “never think about water again.”

Noise, heat, and being a decent neighbor

Portable units are indoor appliances. Their compressors live with you. Listings that say 50–54 dB are in the range of a conversation or a loud refrigerator; that is enough to change how you watch a show or take a call. If you sleep in the same room, look at night modes and at placing the unit so the hose still works but the exhaust side is not aimed at the pillow.

The hose and the window panel dump heat and some noise outward. In a tight courtyard, that heat becomes someone else’s problem. Aim the exhaust toward open air, not into a neighbor’s window three feet away, and do not run a dying unit that rattles all night if you share a wall.

Window units move more of that noise outside, which is better for you and potentially worse for the courtyard. The same courtesy applies.

What about doing nothing to the window?

People try to pinch a hose in a barely-open sash without a kit, or to vent into a hallway. The first wastes most of the cooling and can pinch or melt a hose. The second moves heat to a shared space and can be against building rules. The kit is not optional decoration. It is the difference between air conditioning and an expensive fan that also happens to heat the ceiling.

A portable AC is also not a heater you forget about in January, unless the model truly has a heat-pump mode you intended to buy. Store it upright if the manual says so, and drain it before it sits in a closet.

A renter’s buying path

  1. Read the lease and check the outlet.
  2. Measure the room and the window opening, including whether it slides vertically or horizontally.
  3. If a portable unit is allowed, choose a portable air conditioner whose BTU range matches the room, with a kit that fits your window direction.
  4. Buy extra foam strips and a security bar at the same time so the window does not sit half-solved on the hottest day of the year.
  5. If you have a slider, add a vertical kit to the cart rather than improvising cardboard.
  6. Photograph the window before and after so you can show that the frame was not screwed.

If the apartment is only miserable at night in one room, size for that room. If you work from home in a glass box all afternoon, size for that room instead. One honest target beats one machine in the hallway trying to cool a lifestyle.

For heat that arrives with light — a west window that also ruins sleep — pair this project with renter-friendly blackout. Blocking sun on the glass reduces how hard the machine has to work, which is the closest thing a renter gets to free cooling.

Frequently asked questions

Are portable air conditioners allowed in apartments?

Often yes, because they vent through a temporary window kit instead of hanging a box from the frame. Your lease still wins. Some buildings ban all window modifications or have electrical limits.

Do I need to drill to install a portable AC window kit?

No. Most kits are meant to be cut to width and held with foam, unfolding panels, or tension. If a kit suggests screws into the frame, use the included friction pieces or extra foam instead.

Why does my portable AC make the room feel sticky?

A single-hose unit exhausts air from the room and can pull warm, humid air back in from the rest of the apartment. A short, straight hose and a dual-hose design reduce that fight.
